# The Zarankiewicz number $Z(16,17;3,3)=132$

The maximum number of ones in a $16\times17$ binary matrix without an all-one $3\times3$ submatrix is 132. The lower bound is known. The argument below excludes a 133-entry matrix using degree counts, local packing bounds, and finite clique checks. [Afrasyab, Theorem 1.1](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.08154v1) · [Collins, Riasanovsky, Wallace and Radziszowski, Table 4](https://www.cs.rit.edu/~spr/PUBL/crwr16.pdf)

An earlier computational result showed that any hypothetical 133-entry matrix has maximum row degree at most ten. The upper-bound proof below includes its own row-degree reduction and also excludes the remaining profiles. [Slop Dealer Research, MF-186](https://slopdealer.com/research/papers/mf-186/)

## The lower bound

Index the rows and the first 16 columns by $(a,b),(x,t)\in\mathbb F_2^3\times\mathbb F_2$. Set an entry to 1 when $b=t+a\cdot x$. In the extra column put ones at both $b$'s for each of $a=000,001$. This gives $16\cdot8+4=132$. If two rows among three share $a$, their first 16 entries have disjoint support. Otherwise the three distinct $a$'s yield two independent conditions on $x$, giving exactly two common columns among the first 16. Such a triple cannot be contained in the extra column. The construction avoids the forbidden submatrix.

## Local packing bounds

It suffices to rule out exactly 133 ones, since deleting ones cannot create a forbidden submatrix. Suppose there are 133 ones, with row degrees $r_i$ and column degrees $c_j$. The published bounds $Z(15,17)\le126,\ Z(14,17)\le118,\ Z(16,16)\le128$ give minimum row degree 7, at most one degree-7 row, and minimum column degree 5 by deleting one row, two rows, or one column, respectively. For two columns $j,k$, each other column meets their common rows at most twice, hence $\sum_{i\in j\cap k}(r_i-2)\le30$. Likewise for any two rows $a,b$, $\sum_{j\in a\cap b}(c_j-2)\le28$. Six common rows would contribute at least $5+5\cdot6=35>30$, since at most one has degree seven. Thus two columns share at most five rows.

Intersect the supports of all other rows with a fixed row $H$, producing 15 blocks with pairwise intersections at most two. Their total size is $S_H=\sum_{j\in H}(c_j-1)$. For a fixed column $J$, the 16 corresponding blocks have size at most five and sum $S_J=\sum_{i\in J}(r_i-1)$.

For $u$ five-element blocks on $v$ points, the multiplicities $a_x$ satisfy $\sum a_x=5u$ and $\sum\binom{a_x}{2}\le2\binom u2$, so $25u^2/v\le2u^2+3u$. On nine points $u\le3$. The two-block intersection is one or two. With three blocks, integer convexity forces six multiplicities two and three multiplicities one, and each pair meets twice, giving a unique pattern up to relabeling. For one 5-block, two meeting once, two meeting twice, and three 5-blocks, the maximum numbers of 4-blocks are $13,12,10,6$, respectively. Each chosen block meets the fixed blocks and every other chosen block in at most two points. On ten points $u\le6$. For $u=5$, the multiplicities must be $2^5,3^5$, and for $u=6$ they are $3^{10}$. Every pair meets twice by equality in the integer lower bound. With the 5-blocks labelled, enumeration of the multiset of point-membership patterns gives 12 cases for each value of $u$, with at most 7 or 5 compatible 4-blocks, respectively. The supplied verifier generates all membership multisets and checks the clique bounds.

On nine points with $b$ blocks and largest size at most five, the total is at most $3b+2u+\min(b-u,m_u)$ for the four cases above, and at most $4b$ if $u=0$. If there is a 6-block, any 5-block must use all three outside points, and at most one block can use all three. There cannot be a second 6-block, since two would share at least three points. A 4-block not of that kind contains an outside pair and an inside pair. For each of three outside pairs the inside pairs are disjoint, giving no more than nine such 4-blocks. Allowing one block of size five to use all three outside points gives the bound $6+5+4\min(9,b-2)+3\max(0,b-11)$. If no block uses all three, the corresponding bound $6+4\min(9,b-1)+3\max(0,b-10)$ is smaller. With a 7-, 8- or 9-block the bounds are $7+4\min(3,b-1)+3\max(0,b-4)$, $8+3(b-1)$, and $9+2(b-1)$, by the same outside-point count. For $b=15,14,13,12,11,10$ and minimum point multiplicity at least $4,3,2,1,0,0$, respectively, these give total bounds $60,57,54,50,47,43$. The only excess values before using the multiplicity hypothesis are 61 and 58 for $b=15,14$. They require two 5-blocks meeting at a point and twelve 4-blocks, and at most one remaining block. The common point cannot be in a 4-block because the two 5-blocks cover all nine points and then its combined intersection size would be five. Its multiplicity is too small.

On ten points any 15 blocks have total at most 64. If all sizes are at most five, this is immediate for at most four 5-blocks and follows from the preceding finite checks for five or six. A block of size 8, 9 or 10 bounds every other size by 4, 3 or 2. A 7-block allows at most one 5-block (all three outside points are required). Two 6-blocks cover the ground set and every further block has size at most four. With exactly one 6-block $F$, any 5-block using all four outside points would exclude every other 5-block. Otherwise the 5-blocks use distinct outside triples $W\setminus\{w_i\}$ and pairwise disjoint inside pairs $P_i$, since any two outside triples already intersect in two points. There are at most three disjoint inside pairs. The only potential excess therefore uses three 5-blocks and eleven 4-blocks. A compatible 4-block must have two inside and two outside points: with three outside points it hits some fixed triple, or its inside point hits the appropriate $P_i$ in addition to two outside points. An outside pair must use two of $w_1,w_2,w_3$ and then take one inside point from each corresponding $P_i$. The inside pairs for a fixed outside pair must be disjoint. The three eligible outside pairs give at most $3\cdot2=6$ such 4-blocks, contradicting eleven.

## Column degrees and row types

Deleting each block in turn gives total size at most $\lfloor16\cdot64/15\rfloor=68$ for 16 blocks on ten points. Any fixed ten rows of a column of degree at least ten would give at least $9\cdot7+6=69$ entries on those rows after deleting the column. Thus every column degree is at most nine. For a degree-9 column $J$ the five-block bounds on its 16 blocks give $S_J\le64$. At equality they are all 4-blocks. The only alternative requires two 5-blocks meeting once, twelve 4-blocks and two 3-blocks, so the common point has multiplicity at most four, below the minimum multiplicity $r_i-1\ge6$. Then each point has multiplicity at most eight, since a pair of points belongs to at most three 4-blocks (the remaining pairs are disjoint) and $3a_x\le8\cdot3$. If $J$ contained a row of degree at least ten, it would give $S_J\ge9+8\cdot7-1=64$ and multiplicity at least nine, a contradiction.

For $S_J=63$, the only additional patterns allowed by the packing counts are $5^2,4^{12},3,2$ and $5^2,4^{11},3^3$, both with two 5-blocks meeting once. The common point has multiplicity at most five, so the surviving patterns are $4^{15},3$ and $5,4^{13},3^2$. In particular there is at most one intersection of size five and at most two of size three, none below three. When all nine row degrees are eight, the second pattern is impossible: the 5-block must meet each of the other 15 blocks twice, since $5(7-1)=30$. For each pair of outside points at most two of the 4-blocks can use it, so there are at most twelve. Thus in this case the intersections are fifteen fours and one three.

Write high and low rows for degrees nine and eight, and $L$ for the possible degree-7 row. A degree-9 column contains no larger-degree row. Its types are $N_0,N_1,C_0,C_1,C_2$, where $N$ omits $L$, $C$ contains it, and the subscript is the number of high rows. This follows from $S_J=63+h-[L\in J]\le64$. A column of type $N_1,C_2$ meets every other column in exactly four rows. A column of type $C_1$ has at most one intersection of size five, and type $C_0$ has at most two since three 5-blocks give total at most 60.

Let $t$ be the number of degree-9 columns. The positive column deficits $\delta_j=8-c_j$ sum to $t+3$. A high row containing $a$ degree-9 columns needs incident positive deficits at least $3+a$, by its local bound $S_H\le60$. A row $R$ of degree $r\ge10$ satisfies $8r-20\le S_R\le\lfloor32r/5\rfloor$. The lower bound subtracts the contribution of the other $17-r$ columns, and the upper bound averages the ten-point bound over all ten-subsets of its support. Thus $r\le12$. It avoids degree-9 columns, requiring incident deficit at least $7r-\lfloor32r/5\rfloor=\lceil3r/5\rceil$, so $t\ge3,4,5$ at $r=10,11,12$. Two such rows have common size $m\ge t+3$, contradicting $28\ge6m-(t+3)\ge30$.

If a large row exists and $t\ge5$, choose five degree-9 columns on the remaining 15 rows. Their pair intersections total at least 45 by integer convexity. Pairs of intersection size five form a graph of maximum degree two (and only $C_0$ has degree two), so the total is at most $4\binom52+5=45$. Equality gives every row multiplicity three and every column type $C_0$, forcing $L$ to have multiplicity five, impossible. Thus $t\le4$ and no row has degree 12. A degree-11 row requires $t=4$, with pair overlap at least 27 from 36 entries on 15 rows. If $L$ is absent all pair intersections are at most four. If $L$ is present there are eleven low rows and at most one $C_0$ since two $C_0$'s would share $L$ and five low rows. The graph of 5-intersections has at most two edges, giving total at most 26. Hence the remaining degree profiles are $A=(9^5,8^{11})$, $B=(7,9^6,8^9)$, $C=(10,9^3,8^{12})$, and $D=(7,10,9^4,8^{10})$.

## Eliminate profiles $C,D,B$

In $C$, $t\ge3$. A column $N_0$ cannot coexist with $N_0$ or $N_1$ by their overlaps among 12 low rows. The $N_1$'s have distinct high rows, so $t=3$, and their omitted low rows partition the 12 low rows into three groups of four. The weights of each high and low row across these columns are 1 and 2. All six positive deficit units are on $R$ by the large-row bound. Each of the four columns avoiding $R$ and distinct from the three fixed columns has degree eight and total overlap 12 with those columns. This requires four rows of weight one, but there are only three.

In $D$ the overlap bounds among ten low rows exclude $N_0,C_0$ in a collection of at least three and allow at most one $N$ column, because two such columns would share six low rows. An $N_1$ cannot coexist with $C_1$. If $N_1$ is present the other columns are $C_2$. Their high pairs avoid its high row and must be distinct. Any two $C_2$ high pairs then share a row, so their four-element low complements are disjoint, as is the two-element $N_1$ low complement. This permits exactly two $C_2$'s. The fixed three columns give weight one to exactly three high rows, weight two to other rows outside $R$, and weight zero to $R$. The argument for $C$ excludes this.
If there is no $N_1$, two $C_1$'s must have distinct high rows and share five rows, so at most two occur. The high pairs of $C_2$'s avoid the $C_1$ high rows and their low complements are disjoint from the $C_1$ low complements. Two $C_2$'s have distinct high pairs. Their four-element low complements intersect in zero points if the pairs meet, and one if the pairs are disjoint, by the exact overlap four. Three distinct high pairs would form a star, triangle or three-edge path, requiring low-complement union of at least eleven. Thus there are at most two $C_2$'s, and at least one $C_1$. The case $C_1+2C_2$ requires disjoint low complements of sizes $3,4,4$, impossible. In $2C_1+C_2$ the high rows are partitioned and the low complements partition the ten low rows. Weights are 1 on high rows, 2 on low rows, 3 on $L$, 0 on $R$. For a degree-8 column off $R$ outside this triple the overlap sum is at most 12, since the two $C_1$'s use their unique five-intersection with one another. Its overlap sum is $16+\ell-p$, with $\ell$ the incidence with $L$ and $p$ the number of high rows. Hence all four such degree-8 columns would contain the four high rows, impossible.

In $B$, with nine low rows, any collection of at least two degree-9 columns can only use $C_2$'s, by minimum overlap of the low supports and the exact overlap four for $N_1,C_2$. Two $C_2$'s have disjoint high pairs and disjoint low complements, hence $t\le3$. If $t=3$, the weights are 1 on high rows, 2 on low rows, 3 on $L$. A nonfixed column of degree $c$ contains $p=2c-12+\ell$ high rows by overlap sum 12. Degree 5 is impossible, and degree 6 or 7 contains at most three high rows. The available weighted deficit is at most $3\cdot6=18$ and demand at least $6\cdot4=24$.
If $t=2$, four high rows $P_1$ belong to one fixed column and two $P_0$ to none. A nonfixed column with $h$ high rows and $h_0$ from $P_0$ satisfies $h+h_0\le2c-8$ by overlap sum eight. Demand is $\sum\delta h\ge22,\ \sum\delta h_0\ge6$, so $28\le40-2\sum\delta^2$. Deficits sum to five, forcing all five to be one. The four $P_1$ rows use exactly four of these five columns, with distinct omitted columns, since any triple sharing three would be forbidden. Each of the five columns therefore contains at least three $P_1$'s and has $h+h_0\le6$, allowing at most one $P_0$. The two $P_0$'s need at least six incidences, impossible.
If $t=0$, all six high rows must occur in all positive-deficit columns. The partitions $1+1+1,2+1,3$ of deficit three respectively force a forbidden submatrix, violate the two-column bound, or require too many rows in a degree-5 column.
If $t=1$, an outside high row needs deficit at least three. For the fixed column $J$, the supply is at most $\sum_j\delta_j(c_j-|J\cap j|)$. For $N_0$, demand is 18 and supply at most 13 using one intersection three and the others four. For $N_1$, demand is 15 and supply at most $16-\sum\delta_j^2\le12$. For $C_1$, demand is 15 and its at most two 3-intersections add at most the two largest deficits to $16-\sum\delta_j^2$. For the four partitions $1+1+1+1,2+1+1,2+2,3+1$ the resulting bounds are $14,13,12,10$. For $C_2$, demand 12 forces four unit deficits and equality. The two inside high rows must be on all four deficit columns, and an outside high row occurs with them in at least three, impossible. Finally $C_0$ has demand 18, local size 62 and minimum multiplicity six. Removing $1,2,3,4$ local blocks leaves sizes at most $60,57,54,50$ by the local bounds. Thus the removed totals are at least $2,5,8,12$. For the four deficit partitions this gives weighted intersections at least $12,10,10,9$, whereas $\sum\delta_j c_j=28,26,24,22$, respectively. All supply bounds are below 18.

## Profile $A$ and the two final prefixes

In $A$ at most one degree-9 column occurs by overlaps among eleven low rows. An $N_0$ fails demand 15 against supply at most 13 as above. For an $N_1$ with high row $H_0$, the outside four high rows require deficit 12, so the supply bound $16-\sum\delta^2$ forces four unit deficits. Each of those columns contains $H_0$ and three outside high rows. The outside rows miss distinct columns, or two of them would share three columns with $H_0$. Every other column containing $H_0$ and of degree eight may contain at most one outside high row, since any pair of them already shares two columns with $H_0$. Such a column must include the three outside low rows to meet the fixed column in four. The row $H_0$ meets the fixed degree-9 column and all four deficit columns. Its other four columns would therefore share the three outside low rows, giving a forbidden submatrix. Thus $t=0$. All five high rows lie in every deficit column, excluding $1+1+1$ by a forbidden submatrix and $2+1$ by the two-column bound $5\cdot7>30$. The only deficit column has degree five and supports the five high rows $H$.

Delete this column to obtain a regular $16\times16$ matrix $A$ of degree eight. Every row pair shares at most four columns, since its $q$ common columns use $6q\le28$ ones on other rows. Put $L_{ij}=4-|A_i\cap A_j|$ off the diagonal, zero on it. Each row of $L$ sums to four. For the sign matrix $W=2A-\mathbf1$, $WW^T=16I-4L$. For $i\in H$ set $\epsilon_i=\sum_{j\notin H}L_{ij}$. In the original matrix row $i$ has local total 60 and multiplicities $4,7^8$. The nine-point bounds permit local block types $4^{15},\ 5,4^{13},3,$ or $5^2,4^{11},3^2$, with two 5-blocks meeting at one point. The alternative $5^2,4^{12},2$ cannot meet the minimum multiplicity at the common point. A 5-block must come from another row of $H$. In the last case the common point can occur at most four times and is therefore the removed column, so both 3-blocks also come from $H$. Consequently $\epsilon_i\le1$.

The sum $\epsilon=\sum_{i\in H}\epsilon_i=20-2\sum_{\{i,j\}\subset H}L_{ij}$ is even and at most four. The Gram identity gives $\|\sum_{i\in H}W_i\|^2=4\epsilon$. The 16 coordinates are odd, so equality holds with $\epsilon=4$ and all coordinates $\pm1$. Exactly eight columns contain two distinguished rows and eight contain three. Those $i$ with $\epsilon_i=1$ have internal deficits $0,1,1,1$ (the lone 3-block is outside and the 5-block inside). The remaining row has total internal deficit four and all its pair deficits are at most one by symmetry, hence all four are one. The internal deficit graph is therefore $K_5$ minus a matching of size two.

The eight triple column patterns are distinct, because any repeated triple plus the deleted column would be forbidden. Let $m_i,m_{ij}$ count the two omitted triples containing $i$ or $\{i,j\}$, respectively. A pair pattern occurs $m_{ij}+\mathbf1_{\{i,j\}\text{ in matching}}$ times by its overlap requirement. Row size eight then gives $m_i+\deg_{\rm matching}(i)=2$. Relabeling, the omitted triples are $012,034$, and the matching is $12,34$ or $13,24$. These produce exactly the two 5-row prefixes encoded in the verifier.

The total cross deficit is four, so at least seven of the eleven remaining rows have intersection four with each distinguished row. Any such row is an 8-subset meeting every pair overlap of distinguished rows in at most two. Two such rows can coexist only if their intersection has size at most four and meets each distinguished row in at most two. For each prefix the verifier generates all $\binom{16}{8}$ row candidates, tests these conditions, and enumerates all cliques up to size seven. There are 86 and 60 vertices, respectively. Neither graph contains a 7-clique, contradicting the seven rows required and completing the proof.
